## ch1_s3a | A Room With Two Witnesses | lantern_room | dusk | ada,tomas
narrator: After the second line stops forming, neither of you moves toward the machine. The pause is not agreement. It is two people discovering that astonishment has briefly made them poor at deciding where to put their hands.
tomas: First, we stand somewhere that isn't a staircase.
ada: Good first principle.
narrator: You move to the broad landing. Tomas brings the camera, leaving the recorder where it is. He checks that the tower door opens from inside, then points out the unobstructed passage to the house. The gestures are plain and grounding. An exit does not solve a mystery, but knowing where it is makes the mystery less capable of pretending to own you.
ada: You said you ration surprise.
tomas: I may have exceeded the allowance.
narrator: You both look back at the paper. The bell sentence is still there. Its continued existence should be reassuring; instead, you are aware of how quickly your mind has begun treating it as a person who might answer another question.
ada: Should we ask it something?
tomas: With what mechanism?
ada: I don't know. I was about to speak at it.
tomas: You can. But speaking at a printer doesn't usually improve its manners.
narrator: The joke makes the impulse visible enough to resist. There is a receiving stylus, paper, and several pipes. You have not identified an input. You do not know whether the machine responds to sound, touch, pressure, or some process hidden elsewhere. Calling a question into the room would be an action, but not necessarily an experiment.
ada: Let's list what actually happened before we start a conversation with plumbing.
tomas: Much better.
narrator: You read the observations aloud. The first line formed at 19:58. It named a cup, a time tomorrow, and a handle. The second formed at 20:03. It named a falling bell and an outcome. Both were produced by the same visible stylus. Both resembled your handwriting. Neither line named its author.
tomas: And I had been in the tower before you arrived. There was another short pattern at 18:35. I recorded the feed moving because I thought the mechanism was sticking. That's on video, with the clock in frame.
ada: So we have an earlier mark to compare if we find what drives it.
tomas: Yes. We don't have to decide tonight what it means.
narrator: You inspect the room from a distance. No one has left a projector, computer, or ordinary printer beside the mechanism. That does not establish their absence from the building. You photograph the visible connections and make a list of areas you have not inspected, including the sealed housing and the pipe below the floor.
ada: My first instinct is that Ivo arranged it.
tomas: Mine too. Familiar person, unfamiliar apparatus. The mind prefers a known culprit.
ada: That sounds unfair to him.
tomas: It could be. It could also be right. We earn the conclusion either way.
narrator: The tower wall bears several old service labels. Some have been crossed out and replaced. One dates a bearing replacement. Another warns against operating a control while the shaft is occupied. A newer tag says the bell's mechanical release is isolated pending inspection. Tomas photographs the tags without touching the controls.
ada: Does the line about the bell make you want to check it or stay away from it?
tomas: Both. So I suggest someone qualified checks it while we stay away. An unexpectedly satisfying compromise.
narrator: You send Nessa a factual message: unusual paper output witnessed, no injuries, tower shaft requires professional inspection before any operation. You include the exact bell wording in a photograph. You do not write that the lighthouse has predicted a death, because it has not. You do not write that it has guaranteed safety, because the line's origin remains unknown.
tomas: That's a good message.
ada: It took three drafts to remove the part where I sound like a person in a bad film.
tomas: That part is allowed in a private notebook.
narrator: You open a new note and write: I am frightened by my own handwriting. It is the first sentence of the evening that does not pretend to be evidence. You leave it there, separate from the incident record. The division is not a denial of the feeling. It gives the feeling a place where it does not have to prove anything.
ada: Do you ever get tired of being exact?
tomas: Constantly. Mostly when I want someone to hear the emotional version first. But if I'm not exact with a claim, the person who disagrees can attack the exaggeration and ignore the thing that actually happened.
ada: You learned that here.
tomas: Among other places.
narrator: There is a faint scuff on the landing where a chair once stood. You imagine Ivo sitting there, watching the paper. Then you correct the sentence inside your head: the mark could have come from a stool, a box, or equipment dragged across the floor. You cannot spend the whole visit policing every image your mind offers. You can refrain from putting those images in the catalogue.
narrator: Tomas shows you the earlier pattern video. Three strokes, then a circle with a flattened side. His recorded voice says the feed appears active despite the stationary face dial. He sounds irritated rather than astonished. The difference between his tone then and now makes the present feel more real.
ada: Keep the irritated commentary.
tomas: It's my finest technical work.
narrator: He makes a copy of both videos and notes where the original files remain. You do the same with your photographs. Neither copy makes the observation true by repetition. It does make it less vulnerable to one dropped phone.
narrator: Nessa replies that a rigger will inspect the bell supports and shaft mechanism, and that neither of you should operate anything before then. You read the message aloud. Tomas says understood, even though she is not on the line to hear it.
ada: Habit?
tomas: The good kind, I hope.
narrator: You turn off the extra work light, leaving the fixed service lamp on. The paper becomes less theatrical when it occupies only one small part of the room. The stairs, pipes, patched floor, and cold window remain. The building contains a mystery. It is not made entirely of one.
narrator: When Tomas suggests listening to an ordinary tape at the archive, you agree quickly. You want a voice whose route to the present can be traced through something as comprehensible as a spool of magnetic material. You take one final photograph of the room as you leave, including the locked shaft gate and the clear exit, so tomorrow's record will remember the things fear might otherwise edit out.
## ch1_s4a | The Ordinary Track | archive | evening | ada,tomas
narrator: Tomas does not immediately put the house log away. He rewinds to a section before Ivo mentioned your letter and asks whether you want to hear the end of a domestic argument about a radio. You consider saying no because you are tired. Then you realize you are tired of the mystery, not necessarily of the voice.
ada: If it doesn't reveal the secret history of the kettle.
tomas: I can offer no guarantee. Appliances have been carrying this archive for years.
narrator: On the tape, Ivo tries to tune a small radio. A presenter fades in and out beneath static. He complains that the station has moved its frequency, then realizes he is using the shortwave band. There is a long silence, followed by a very quiet correction addressed to nobody.
ivo: Operator error. Regrettably.
narrator: You laugh before the phrase is finished. It is not because the joke is especially good. It is because you can see the exact expression he would have used: annoyed at himself, more annoyed at the absence of someone who might appreciate his annoyance.
ada: He used to say that when he burned toast.
tomas: Did he burn a lot of toast?
ada: He insisted the toaster's markings were a form of misleading advertising. I don't think he ever considered using a lower setting consistently.
narrator: Tomas lets the recording continue. A fragment of music emerges, distant and distorted. Ivo stops moving around the room. For almost a minute, all you hear is the song and the soft mechanical noise of the recorder. Then the reception fails and he turns the radio off.
ivo: Must ask about an outside aerial. No point pretending the house is better insulated than it is.
narrator: The song is one your mother used to play while cleaning. You cannot remember its title. You remember her opening a window on cold mornings and telling you that a room had to be allowed to change its mind about the air. Ivo must have known the song too. The recognition creates a bridge you did not ask for and cannot entirely resent.
ada: Can you identify music from a fragment?
tomas: Sometimes. Not by magic. Melody, lyrics if they're clear, recording date, broadcast listings if they survive. We can make a note for later.
ada: Later is good.
narrator: He writes the timestamp on a card and gives it to you. No promise to solve it tonight. No suggestion that finding the title will repair your relationship with the dead. Just a small question saved in a form that will not be lost among the larger ones.
narrator: You ask why he chose audio conservation rather than recording new music. He explains that new music requires being pleasant to musicians at moments when they are frightened of themselves, and that he respects people who can do it well. Old recordings require a different patience. The performance has already happened; his job is to avoid adding a new mistake.
ada: That sounds almost peaceful.
tomas: Some days. Other days, a tape sheds oxide into the machine and I spend an hour using words my father would have admired.
ada: Did he teach you the work?
tomas: No. He taught me to listen to engines, which isn't entirely different. He could tell when a pump was unhappy by the way the wall carried its vibration. I wanted something less oily. The universe gave me magnetic tape adhesive instead.
narrator: He shows you a reel damaged by poor storage, not one connected to the inquiry. Its edges curl slightly, and a brown residue marks the leader. He explains what he can stabilize, what he might recover, and what may remain lost. He does not describe loss as a failure of care when the damage occurred before anyone asked for help.
ada: That's a useful distinction.
tomas: I try to remember it outside the workshop. With mixed success.
narrator: On a shelf near the desk, a small repaired speaker plays a calibration tone barely louder than a refrigerator. You ask whether its patched cloth changes the sound. Tomas says slightly, then asks whether you think the patch should be hidden if the speaker becomes part of an exhibit.
ada: Depends on the exhibit's purpose. If the repair tells part of the object's history, hiding it might make the object look less truthful. But visible damage isn't automatically more authentic. Sometimes that's a style people impose on old things.
tomas: Thank you. I have been trying to explain that to a donor who wants everything to sound like it was recorded in a cupboard full of rain.
ada: Nostalgia with adjustable hiss.
tomas: Precisely.
narrator: The conversation becomes technical for a while in a way that feels restful. You discuss adhesive reversibility, cataloguing uncertain dates, the ethics of removing a noise that may be historically meaningful. Neither of you needs to prove the work matters. You have both chosen lives in which small changes can alter what somebody later believes they encountered.
narrator: Then Tomas asks whether you liked Ivo. The directness surprises you. He does not ask whether you loved him, which would be both easier and less informative.
ada: Sometimes very much. Sometimes I spent a whole visit waiting for him to notice he was making everyone work around his moods. He could be wonderful at teaching a thing and terrible at being asked for something he hadn't decided to give.
tomas: That sounds familiar.
ada: I wanted him to think I was good at my work. I disliked wanting it. Both were true even after I was old enough to know better.
narrator: Tomas nods without treating the admission as childish. Approval can retain its importance long after a person understands how arbitrarily it is distributed.
tomas: I wanted him to tell me what was in the missing part of the record. He gave me tools, loaned equipment, remembered things about my father. Every kindness made me think the answer was closer. Then another year passed.
ada: I'm sorry he left you with that.
tomas: Thank you. You don't have to answer for it.
narrator: You let the distinction stand. It is possible to be sorry without becoming responsible for repairing every consequence. You suspect both of you will need to practice that more than once before the inventory ends.
narrator: Tomas stops the tape at its original end, preserving the long tail of room noise. He rewinds it, writes the playback date in the log, and returns it to its case. The voice disappears into an ordinary object. You are startled by how comforting that is after the tower.
narrator: At the door, he gives you the card with the unidentified song's timestamp. You put it in your pocket beside the grocery receipt. Two small records of a day that is already becoming difficult to describe. The lighthouse waits up the hill, but for the walk back you have a tune in your head rather than a sentence on a strip.
